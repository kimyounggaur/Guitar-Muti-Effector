# Factory preset output calibration

Measured in Chromium with `OfflineAudioContext` at 44,100 Hz, before the preset trim, output safety limiter and master volume. The test signal is a deterministic 2.5-second mono sequence of four synthesised plucked tones at approximately 82, 110, 147 and 196 Hz. It peaks around -12.8 dBFS; this is a repeatable probe, not a recording of a guitar. The measurement covers the noise gate and compressor worklets and the enabled pedal effects, including the installed cabinet responses. The looper and rhythm are stopped, and the tuner is transparent.

`audio-calibration.html` runs the measurement using the application effects; `audio-calibration-diagnostic.html?index=71` exposes the stages of a selected factory preset. The complete RMS and peak measurements are in `src/store/factoryCalibration.ts`, arranged in factory category and patch order. Experimental presets were measured again after reducing extreme bias, compression, EQ cuts and wet levels, and replacing an unconfigured custom cabinet with an internal cabinet. No master settings are changed by this calibration.

For each patch, the trim uses the smaller of 0 dB, the attenuation needed to approach -45 dBFS RMS, and that needed to keep the measured peak at or below -8 dBFS. Attenuation is limited to 36 dB; this never boosts an individual preset. With this particular test signal the measured preset range narrowed from 46.35 dB to 13.76 dB; 86 of 108 patches ended within 3 dB of the -45 dBFS test target. The largest applied attenuation is 32.59 dB. This target is selected for relative balancing and is not a recommended listening level.

The remaining range includes intentionally spacious presets whose cabinet, modulation, delay and reverb together attenuate this test signal. For example, the diagnostic for Ambient 12 measured approximately -24.39 dBFS at input and -60.03 dBFS at the final stage when monitored through the diagnostic's channel taps. These values differ slightly from the main stereo measurement because the diagnostic taps a single channel. Different notes, pickups, interfaces and playing dynamics can change both the RMS and peak considerably. This experiment does not replace listening, loudness normalization across every source, limiter validation, or tests with real instruments and phones.
